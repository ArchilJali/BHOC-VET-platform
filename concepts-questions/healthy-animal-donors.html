Research Concept · Veterinary transfusion · Donor welfare

Do we have the right to depend on healthy animal donors?

Veterinary blood donation saves lives. The question is not whether donor blood is valuable. The question is whether every oxygen-delivery emergency should continue to depend entirely on another healthy animal being available to donate.

Concept & Question · Source-linked analysis · 14 Sep 2026

A safe and available blood supply is essential in veterinary medicine. In May 2026, Mars Veterinary Health and the American Red Cross expanded joint human-and-pet blood drives, while Mars Veterinary Health described a continuing critical shortage of veterinary blood products.1 This is an important response to an immediate clinical need. It also exposes a deeper systems question.

Blood donation saves the recipient. What does it require from the donor?

Human blood donation is voluntary and based on informed consent. Veterinary donors cannot make that decision themselves. Their participation depends on owners, veterinary teams, donor selection, handling and the ability to perform collection with an acceptable welfare and safety profile.

This is particularly relevant in feline transfusion medicine. The 2021 ISFM Consensus Guidelines state that blood collection can be performed in conscious cats with a skilled team, but that movement and signs of anxiety are more frequent in conscious donors and that sedation or general anaesthesia is commonly used. The guidelines conclude that donors should, in most cases, be sedated appropriately for blood collection.2

29,201feline donation events

Retrospective study across 7,812 individual cats between 2019 and 2023.3

95.7%of donation events involved sedation

27,925 of 29,201 events were recorded in the sedated group in the published dataset.3

4.4×higher odds of an adverse reaction in conscious donors

Overall adverse reactions were uncommon at 0.29%, but conscious donation was the significant identified risk factor.3

These data should not be misread. They do not show that feline blood donation is generally unsafe. The study found a low overall adverse-reaction rate, and appropriate sedation was associated with lower risk. But they do show that obtaining blood from a healthy cat is not a biologically neutral event. It can require chemical restraint, venepuncture, monitoring and recovery in an animal that was healthy before the procedure.

If another technology can reduce our dependence on healthy animal donors, do we have an obligation to investigate it?

The problem is larger than donation alone.

Emergency transfusion depends on more than having a willing donor. The system must also provide the right product for the right species and recipient, at the right location and at the right time. Blood typing, cross-matching, infectious-disease screening, collection, processing, storage and transport all protect the patient, but they also create unavoidable operational constraints.

For a critically ill animal, the physiological problem may develop faster than the blood-supply chain can respond. The immediate objective in that interval is not to reproduce every function of blood. It is to preserve the functions that are failing until definitive treatment becomes available.

A different architecture: donor blood plus an oxygen-delivery reserve.

We believe the useful translational question is not whether donor blood should be replaced. Donor blood performs multiple functions and will remain essential. The question is whether veterinary emergency medicine could add another layer: a donor-independent, shelf-stable oxygen-delivery therapeutic that can be available before compatible donor blood is sourced.

Such a product would need its own species-specific safety, dosing and efficacy evidence. It cannot inherit the approval of a historical product, and oxygen carrying alone does not replace plasma, platelets, immune functions or the full biological architecture of blood.

But if a validated oxygen carrier could bridge a patient through the period when oxygen delivery is compromised, it could potentially change the role of the blood bank from the only immediate option to one component of a broader emergency system.

The concept

Current architecture: emergency need → compatible donor blood must be available → collection / bank / transport → transfusion.

Proposed research architecture: emergency need → immediate oxygen-delivery support when appropriate → compatible donor blood or definitive therapy when required.

Evidence boundary

This concept is not an argument against veterinary blood donation. Donation saves lives and the available literature shows that properly managed feline donation can have a low adverse-reaction rate.

It is also not evidence that BHOC is currently safe or effective across species. It is a research question: whether a future donor-independent oxygen therapeutic could reduce time-critical dependence on compatible animal donors while preserving donor welfare and recipient access.
